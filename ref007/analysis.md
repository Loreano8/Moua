## 1. "If my life as a traveler had a trailer" (35.7 s)
- **Format:** live-action drone and travel footage inside a mocked-up streaming-app interface (logo sting, profile picker, title page, player UI). Music only, no dialogue. 9:16 frame with a letterboxed 16:9 picture.
- **Premise:** an ordinary travel vlog is framed as a streaming series ("WEEKEND VLOG"), which turns real life into "content".
- **Structure:** hook 0–3 (logo sting plus a caption that states the idea) → setup 3–10 (profile select, series page with a tagline; menu clicks over silence) → escalation 10–30 (the music drops in and scenery montages through mountains, a lake, a boat, the coast) → payoff/ending 30–35 (sunset, then it fades on a final wide shot). There's no twist; the payoff is the mood.
- **Rhythm:** ~14 cuts. Pacing goes from slow UI navigation to steady 1.6 s beats that land on the music. Transitions are hard cuts plus a fade at the end, and the UI overlay stays on screen the whole time.
- **Sound:** about 5 s of near-silence with UI clicks, then a cinematic track starts. That contrast is the whole build. The track follows a single emotional arc and has no SFX gags.
- **Text:** one fixed caption at the top for the entire video, plus diegetic UI text (title, rating, tagline). Very little to read.
- **Why it works cold:** the platform UI is universally recognised and instantly says "this is a show", so viewers need no backstory. The images do the work.
- **Weakness:** there's no character, conflict or line of dialogue, so it's pure vibe and forgettable as a story. The same caption sits on screen the whole time, and the ending fades out without a final beat.

## 2. "2000s teen movie trailers be like" (103.7 s)
- **Format:** live action. One performer plays every role through costume swaps (the hero, a referee, a mysterious "time man", a bartender, a mother, a cab driver) against greenscreen and stock-photo backgrounds. Dialogue plus narrator voice-over, in English.
- **Premise:** a teen wishes to be an adult, a magical stranger grants it, and adulthood turns out to be awful, all parodying body-swap and wish comedies.
- **Structure:** hook 0–3 (a top caption names the genre, and the narrator says "[Name] was just your average teen…") → setup 3–24 (overachiever, then the stated desire "I wanna be an adult", then the birthday wish) → inciting event 24–35 (alarm clock, mirror reveal, fake studio card "2000s Productions Presents") → escalation 35–75 (the magic-man rules, the first "adult" experience flops, then a rapid-fire run of no money, no home, kicked out) → stakes line 76 ("This summer, [hero] must…") plus a title card → button 81–101 (taxi gag, jail, cast-name cards, a fake title card, and a last joke after the title, "Coming Never").
- **Rhythm:** ~61 cuts. It's slower during setup (shots of 5–7 s), then speeds up to 0.5–1 s shots in the 35–50 and 62–82 escalation blocks. Hard cuts throughout, with genre-style title cards in pastel gradient frames.
- **Sound:** the narrator VO carries the trailer grammar ("This summer…", "…before it's too late"), and dialogue snippets work as punchlines. Bed music sits under it. Short silences (around 47 s and 84–86 s) land the jokes.
- **Text:** burned-in captions for every line, a genre label at the top for the first few seconds, studio card, tagline card, actor-name cards and the title card, all in one consistent bubbly retro font.
- **Why it works cold:** it lays the standard trailer beats out cleanly (normal life → wish → consequence → "this summer…"), so any viewer can follow. The one-actor costume swaps are a running visual joke.
- **Weakness:** over 100 s, it overstays the joke. The 6–20 s stretch is slow, and the post-title tag drags. Several lines are mumbled or hard to hear.

## 3. "Every Colleen Hoover movie trailer" (62.6 s)
- **Format:** live action on phones in real apartments and streets, with one stock/AI insert (snowy pile-up, a car flying off a cliff). Dialogue plus a first-person VO, in English.
- **Premise:** a woman with a perfect life gets The Call (husband in an accident), the melodrama piles up (affair with his brother, the husband is alive, pregnancy), and it ends in a ridiculous tragedy.
- **Structure:** hook 0–4 (walking down a street plus the VO "I was the girl who had it all, but it only takes one call") → setup 4–12 (phone call, crash insert, best-friend support) → escalation 12–46 (each beat flips the last: new love, "your husband is alive", "the two of you?", a fight) → twist/payoff 46–56 (pregnancy test, "who's the father?", then the husband storms out and a car flies off a cliff) → ending 56–62 (deadpan reaction, then the serif title card "Because of What We Lost").
- **Rhythm:** ~55 cuts, roughly one per second the whole way, the fastest of the six. It barely slows down. A small acceleration through 28–40 s comes from "fake intensity" moves: whip pans, close-ups, a hand slamming a wall.
- **Sound:** sincere piano/strings bed, VO on the trailer beats, dialogue as one-liners. The humour comes from deadpan delivery against dramatic music.
- **Text:** a white-box genre label at the top for the first ~10 s, small subtitles, and **large elegant serif "title phrase" cards over the footage** ("You Do", "Survived", "Then… Died FR", the closing title). Those cards structure the whole piece, and one of them is used for a joke.
- **Why it works cold:** you don't need to know the author. The escalating "and then…" structure plus melodramatic music is legible to anyone, and each twist is a punchline in its own right.
- **Weakness:** with a cast of 4–5 friends and fast cuts, it's hard to track who is who (the friend, the brother, the husband) on a first watch. The cut density leaves little room to feel anything.

## 4. "every horror movie trailer" (61.7 s)
- **Format:** live action, handheld phone footage in a rural farmhouse and its grounds. Mostly music and humming; spoken lines are sparse (Whisper picked up a few, e.g. "Welcome to your new home", "Get him", "Your turn"). Language: English.
- **Premise:** a woman moves into a remote house where a creepy man on a swing and eerie signs build dread, and the "monster" turns out to want someone to play a board game with him.
- **Structure:** hook 0–3 (genre label, car arrival, "Welcome to your new home", cut to black) → setup 3–20 (the house, a figure in the distance, "I think someone else…") → title card at ~20 s ("It's time to play", white serif on black) → escalation 20–50 (lullaby humming, a figure on a swing, a slow approach, a hand on the shoulder, door, bell, screaming, slam cuts) → twist 50–59 (the "killer" is sitting on the floor setting up a board game) → ending (black, a handwritten-style "your turn").
- **Rhythm:** ~30 cuts. It's deliberately slow at first (shots of 2–7 s), then a burst of sub-second cuts between 39 and 50 s, followed by one long held reveal shot. That's classic horror pacing: slow dread, a stutter of cuts, then a hold.
- **Sound:** music builds into an a-cappella "la-la" lullaby (a horror cliché done cheaply), with very little dialogue. The tension comes almost entirely from the audio and the black frames, and the twist is funny because the sound stays dead serious.
- **Text:** a white-box genre label for the first ~2 s, then just two title cards ("It's time to play", "your turn"). Minimal and atmospheric.
- **Why it works cold:** horror grammar (empty field, swing, distant figure, slow approach) is universal, and the twist reframes everything without a word. The ending card pays off the opening card.
- **Weakness:** the middle (20–38 s) repeats the swing and approach beats and drags. Bright daylight footage undercuts the dread, and the twist only works if you catch what's on the floor.

## 5. "Fall Hallmark movie trailers be like" (80.3 s)
- **Format:** live action. One performer plays every role (heroine, rival, the mothers, the father) in wigs, against highly saturated studio or AI-generated autumn sets (café, cosy kitchen, falling leaves). Dialogue plus narrator VO, in English.
- **Premise:** a small-town café owner must save her family pumpkin-spice recipe from a rich buyer's son, and they fall in love. It parodies seasonal romance TV movies.
- **Structure:** hook 0–4 (genre label, then the VO "[Name] had a coffee shop…" with the hero product in hand) → setup 4–16 (the antagonist arrives: "we're buying this place") → conflict 16–36 (mother's guilt trip, "work together", "I am not working with him") → mid-trailer channel logo card at 36–38 → escalation/romance 38–63 (forced proximity, emotional backstory, the father backs out) → payoff 63–73 (confession under fairy lights) → ending 74–80 (actor-name supers, pastel title card).
- **Rhythm:** ~30 cuts, steady 2–4 s shots that let the dialogue breathe; it speeds up only slightly in the romance montage. Hard cuts throughout, plus one full-screen brand-colour card as a chapter break.
- **Sound:** a warm acoustic bed throughout, and the VO handles the act breaks ("Now she'll do anything to…"). The humour comes from clichéd lines delivered straight, plus a running gag where the same product is renamed over and over.
- **Text:** a top genre label, subtitles for every line, a logo card, and serif cast-name supers that overlap the actor, ending on a warm gradient title card.
- **Why it works cold:** the genre has a fixed, predictable beat sheet (save the shop, enemies-to-lovers), so viewers know where it's going and enjoy the clichés landing on time. The colour grade tells you the genre instantly.
- **Weakness:** at 80 s with even pacing, it has no real twist or spike, and the 38–58 s block feels long. One actor playing every role makes some scenes confusing without the wigs.

## 6. "Netflix documentary trailers showing everything except the interview" (63.6 s)
- **Format:** live action, a single subject in a staged interview set (armchair, curtain, lamp), cut with drone/stock establishing shots. There's almost no dialogue: just off-camera questions, mostly music. Language: English.
- **Premise:** a true-crime doc trailer teases a big interview but only shows the "fluff" B-roll around it: sitting down, sipping water, mic setup, a clapperboard, staring.
- **Structure:** hook 0–3 (genre label plus "Can I get you some water?" over the classic interview framing) → setup 3–20 (drone shots, glass close-ups, mic adjustments, clapperboard, title phrases) → escalation 20–55 (more and more trivial inserts: snacks, a notebook, slow head-turns while the music swells) → twist line 54 ("You haven't asked me anything about the murder") → ending 56–63 (empty armchair, title "THE BUNGALOW MURDERS — IN HIS OWN WORDS", then "COMING SOON" on black).
- **Rhythm:** ~25 cuts, evenly spaced at ~2 s throughout, deliberately monotonous. The joke depends on rhythm promising a climax that never arrives. Transitions are hard cuts and black.
- **Sound:** a solemn piano/strings bed and long silences under the dialogue. The few spoken lines are off-camera questions, and the subject's one complaint is the punchline.
- **Text:** a top genre label, then a doc-style serif in all caps, typed on word by word ("A STORY WAITING TO BE…", "IN THEIR OWN WORDS", "EVERY LAST DETAIL", "A LIFETIME IN THE MAKING"), title, and "COMING SOON".
- **Why it works cold:** you can read the documentary look (slow push-ins, B-roll of hands, drone shots) without context, and the gap between gravitas and nothing happening is self-explanatory.
- **Weakness:** the gag is one-note, the middle is repetitive by design and feels slow, and the payoff line is quiet and easy to miss.

## Common patterns (techniques to borrow, not content)
1. **State the premise in the first 1–2 s.** All six put a one-line label or narrator line on screen right away ("X trailers be like…"). Our equivalent is an on-screen or VO line that sets the fictional world before the first cut.
2. **Use trailer grammar as the skeleton:** ordinary world → disruption → "Now [hero] must…" stakes line → escalation montage → title card → **post-title button** (an extra gag after the title, used in v2, v4 and v6). Audiences know this shape, so clever content can ride on it.
3. **Pace in two gears.** Keep the setup slow (2–5 s shots), then accelerate to sub-second cuts in the final third, and land on one held reveal shot or a black frame. The best ones (v4, v2) change speed on purpose; even pacing (v5, v6) feels long.
4. **Let VO carry the structure and dialogue carry the punchlines.** Narrator lines open each act; characters only speak in short, quotable one-liners. Keep a serious music bed under absurd content, because played-straight melodrama is the comic engine.
5. **Use silence and black frames as punctuation.** Short silences before a punchline, and black or title cards between acts (v4 and v6 use these most).
6. **Use big serif "title phrase" cards** (2–4 words, typed on or faded in over footage) as chapter markers. Five of the six use a premium serif for cards and a plain sans for subtitles.
7. **Cheap production, clear genre codes.** Phones, friends, one actor in costumes, greenscreen or AI backdrops. Recognisability comes from genre codes (colour grade, music, framing), not budget.
8. **Turn the twist into a reframe.** The ending recasts what came before (the monster just wants to play, the doc never asks about the murder). Callback the opening line or card on the final card (v4: "time to play" → "your turn").
9. **Sweet spot of ~60 s.** The 60–65 s pieces feel tightest; the 80–104 s ones sag in the middle. A 90 s limit is a ceiling, not a target.
10. **Watch for these weaknesses:** too many look-alike characters without name supers (v3), one-note gags with no escalation (v6), and long mid-sections without a new beat roughly every 5 s (v2, v5).
